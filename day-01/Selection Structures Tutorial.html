Part 1 of 5 Scenario

🔀 Programs that make decisions

You are writing the program for the campus tuck shop till. A till must decide things: Does this customer get a discount? Did they pay enough? Which item did they choose? Every one of those is a selection structure.

Three ways a program can flow

➡️ SequenceDo the steps one after another, top to bottom.
🔀 SelectionAsk a question. Choose a path based on the answer. (today)
🔁 IterationRepeat steps while something is true.
In real life At the taxi rank: IF you have R20 THEN you take the taxi, ELSE you walk. One question, two possible paths, and you only ever take one of them.

Every selection structure has two parts:

  1. A condition: a question whose answer is only ever TRUE or FALSE.
  2. The steps to do for each answer.
In a flowchart, a decision is always a diamond with two arrows leaving it: one for True, one for False.

Part 2 of 5

↪️ IF and IF-ELSE

The two most used selection structures. Use IF when something extra happens only sometimes. Use IF-ELSE when there are two different paths.

One-way selection: IF … THEN

Rule at the till: customers who spend R100 or more get R10 off. Everyone else pays the normal total. If the condition is false, the program simply skips the THEN part.

Two-way selection: IF … THEN … ELSE

Rule at the till: if the customer paid enough, work out the change. Otherwise say "Not enough money". Exactly one of the two branches runs, never both and never neither.

Part 3 of 5

🪜 More than two paths

Real rules often have several levels, or two conditions at once. You handle levels with an ELSE-IF ladder and combined conditions with AND, OR, NOT.

Multi-way selection: the ELSE-IF ladder

Free item promotion: spend R150 or more → combo; R100 or more → drink; R50 or more → sweet; less → nothing. The program tests the conditions from the top and stops at the first one that is true.

⚠ Order matters. Always test the strictest condition first (150 before 100 before 50). If you tested total >= 50 first, a customer who spent R160 would only get a sweet.

Part 4 of 5

🎛️ The CASE structure

When one variable is compared with a list of exact values (a menu choice, a day number, a grade letter), a CASE structure is neater than a long ELSE-IF ladder.

Pick from the menu

The customer types a menu number. The program finds the matching case, does that one, and skips the rest. If nothing matches, the OTHERWISE (default) case catches it.

Always include an OTHERWISE / default case. Users will type something you did not expect.
